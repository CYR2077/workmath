What Is a Good Freelance Rate?

The question is usually asked as "what does everyone else charge", which is the wrong question. Half of everyone else is undercharging, and copying them means copying their problem.

Last updated 18 November 2026

There are two different numbers

Freelancers confuse two things that look similar and behave completely differently.

The floor

The rate at which the business survives: it covers expenses, tax, unpaid admin time, holiday, sick days, and the income you need. Below this number you are subsidising the client with your own savings, and the longer you do it the harder it is to stop.

The price

What you actually quote, which is a judgement about value, urgency, risk, and how much you want the work. It should never be below the floor, and it is usually above it.

Most pricing advice jumps straight to the second number and skips the first, which is why so much of it produces rates that feel bold and still lose money.

Calculating the floor

profit needed = income ÷ (1 − tax rate) revenue needed = profit needed + expenses billable hours = weeks × hours per week × billable % floor rate = revenue needed ÷ billable hours

Target $70,000 net, $9,000 of expenses, a 30% combined tax and contribution rate, 46 working weeks at 40 hours, 60% billable:

  • Profit required: $70,000 ÷ 0.70 = $100,000
  • Revenue required: $100,000 + $9,000 = $109,000
  • Billable hours: 46 × 40 × 0.60 = 1,104
  • Floor rate: $98.73 per hour

Compare that with the number almost everyone starts from: $70,000 ÷ 2,080 = $33.65. The gap is not padding. It is tax, expenses, unpaid holiday, and the 40% of the week that earns nothing.

The billable percentage is the real lever

Utilisation changes the floor more than any other input, and unlike the rate it can be improved without renegotiating anything.

Batching admin, templating proposals, refusing unpaid discovery, and saying no to calls that should have been emails can move you from 50% to 65% — which is worth more than a 20% rate increase, and nobody has to agree to it.

What the market will bear

Once you have a floor, positioning decides the price. Three things move a freelance rate far more than experience does:

  • Specificity. "Copywriter" competes with everyone. "Onboarding email sequences for B2B SaaS" competes with a dozen people and can charge three times as much.
  • Evidence. Two case studies with numbers beat a portfolio of twelve pretty pieces. Clients pay for outcomes they can imagine, not samples they can admire.
  • Scarcity that is real. Available next week is worth more than available tomorrow, which reads as desperate. A four-week lead time is a price signal.

The practical test of whether your rate is too low: are you booked solid with clients you would rather not have? That is not a demand problem, it is a price problem.

Your hourly floor is the internal reference for all of them. A fixed price that works out below the floor is not a strategic discount, it is a loss you have agreed to in advance.

Terms that protect the rate

The number is only half of what you get paid. The other half is whether it arrives, and when.

  1. Deposit of 30–50% before work starts, on any new client.
  2. A written scope with an explicit list of what is not included.
  3. Change requests quoted separately — no silent absorption.
  4. Late payment interest stated on the invoice, and work pausing past terms.
  5. Annual rate review with 30–60 days' notice to existing clients.

Net 60 from a large client is an interest-free loan you are making them. Either price it in, or shorten the terms.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good freelance hourly rate?

There is no universal number, but there is a universal way to find yours: (target income ÷ (1 − tax rate) + expenses) ÷ billable hours. For most independent professionals in developed markets that lands somewhere between $60 and $150 an hour, with the spread explained by expenses, utilisation, and specialisation rather than talent.

Should I charge hourly or per project?

Hourly when scope is unclear, fixed price when it is genuinely defined and you have done similar work before. Fixed price captures the value of your efficiency; it also transfers scope-creep risk to you. Use the hourly floor as the internal reference for every quote.

How much should I add for unpaid admin time?

Do not add a percentage — set the billable share honestly instead. Most freelancers bill 50–70% of the hours they work. Entering 60% in the calculator already accounts for admin, proposals, invoicing, and marketing.

Is it okay to raise rates on existing clients?

Yes, annually. Give 30–60 days' notice, apply the new rate to new work rather than work already quoted, and state a clear date. 5–10% a year is normal and rarely loses clients who value the work; keeping someone on a five-year-old rate is a pay cut every year.

Why is my freelance rate so much higher than my old salary divided by hours?

Because the employer was paying pension, insurance, holiday, sick leave, equipment, and software, and because only part of your week is billable. Dividing a salary by 2,080 hours is the single most common pricing error in freelancing.

What if clients say my rate is too high?

Some will, and that is information rather than a verdict. Compare it against your floor: if the rate is at or above the floor and the client still objects, the work is not a fit. If you are losing every quote, check whether you are selling a commodity, which is a positioning problem rather than a pricing one.
