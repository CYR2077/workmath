Hourly vs Salary: Which Pays More?

The comparison only becomes real when both offers are expressed in the same unit — money per hour actually worked. Almost nobody does that calculation before accepting, which is why the wrong offer gets accepted for the right-sounding reason.

Last updated 18 November 2026

Convert both sides first

A $65,000 salary sounds like more than $28 an hour. Run the numbers on realistic hours and it often is not.

hourly job: $28.00 × 40 hrs × 50 weeks = $56,000 salaried job: $65,000 ÷ (45 hrs × 50 weeks) = $28.89 per hour

A nine-thousand-dollar gap in headline pay becomes a 89-cent gap per hour. If the salaried role actually runs 50-hour weeks, the effective rate drops to $26.00 and the hourly job wins outright.

The unit that matters is total compensation divided by hours actually worked. Not contracted hours. Not 2,080. The hours you will really be present, including the unpaid extras that never appear in the contract.

The three numbers people forget

Cash pay is the easiest part of the comparison and the least interesting. Three other things move the answer more than a dollar an hour does.

Paid time off

Twenty days of paid leave is worth roughly 8% of salary. On $65,000 that is $5,000 of compensation that does not appear in the headline number. An hourly role with no PTO has to pay more per hour just to break even.

Benefits with a cash value

Employer health insurance contributions commonly run $6,000–$9,000 a year for a single person in the US. A 401(k) match at 4% of a $65,000 salary is another $2,600. Add those to the salaried column before comparing.

Overtime eligibility

This is where hourly work can win decisively. A non-exempt hourly employee who works 45 hours a week earns 5 hours at 1.5×. A salaried exempt employee working the same 45 hours earns nothing extra — and in most US states that is entirely lawful.

When hourly is the better deal

  • The overtime is real and regular. A 50-hour week at $25 an hour pays $65,000. The same $65,000 salary at 50 hours is $25 an hour with no premium.
  • You value the boundary. Hourly work creates a financial signal every time someone asks for another hour. Salaried work does not, which is precisely why salaried hours creep.
  • You need cash now. Hourly work pays for the hours you work this week. A salary is smoothed across the year, which helps in slow months and hurts when you want to convert effort into money immediately.
  • The benefits are thin either way. If neither offer includes health insurance, the comparison collapses to pure arithmetic and hourly with overtime usually wins.

When salary is the better deal

  • The hours are genuinely 40. A salary that does not require extra hours is just an hourly job with a different payment schedule, plus paid leave and benefits.
  • Income stability matters. Hourly work in sectors with seasonal demand means weeks of 20 hours that no hourly rate compensates for.
  • The benefits are substantial. Good health cover, a pension contribution, and generous leave can be worth $10,000–$15,000 a year. That is $5–$7 an hour at full time.
  • You want progression. Salaried roles usually sit on a promotion ladder. Hourly roles often top out at a rate ceiling unless you move into supervision.

The questions that settle it

Before deciding, get five numbers in writing. Without them the comparison is guesswork:

  1. What are the guaranteed minimum hours per week, if any?
  2. Is the role exempt or non-exempt for overtime?
  3. How many hours did the last person in this role actually work per week?
  4. What is the employer's contribution to health insurance and retirement, in dollars?
  5. How many days of paid leave accrue per year, and do they carry over?

Question three is the one interviewers answer least honestly and the one that changes the arithmetic most. Ask the person who currently does the job, not the person hiring for it.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert a salary to an hourly rate?

Divide it by the hours you actually work, not by 2,080. A $65,000 salary over 47-hour weeks for 50 weeks is $27.66 an hour; over a nominal 2,080 hours it looks like $31.25. The first number is real, the second is a payroll convention.

Is a salaried job always worse than hourly?

No. If the hours are genuinely around 40 and the benefits are real, a salary usually beats an equivalent hourly rate because of paid leave, health cover, and pension contributions. The problem is that those conditions are less common than job adverts suggest.

Can my employer switch me from hourly to salary to avoid overtime?

Only if the role genuinely meets the exemption tests: salary basis, salary level, and duties. Reclassifying a non-exempt job as salaried without changing the duties does not remove the overtime obligation, and it is a common source of back-pay claims.

What is a good rule of thumb for comparing offers?

Total compensation — cash plus employer benefits plus the cash value of paid leave — divided by realistic annual hours. If the difference is under about 5%, treat the offers as equal on money and decide on schedule, stability, and progression instead.

Do part-time salaried roles exist and how are they compared?

They do, usually at a pro-rated salary. Convert them the same way: annual cash plus pro-rated benefits, divided by the hours actually worked. Partial benefits are the detail people miss — health cover is often all-or-nothing rather than pro-rated.
