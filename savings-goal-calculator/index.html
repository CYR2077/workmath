The two-step calculation

Most savings calculators ask for a future amount and assume it is fixed. That is the wrong frame for a goal you will spend later, because the thing you are buying gets more expensive while you save towards it. Do it in two steps.

Step 1 — inflate the goal to the target date

nominal goal = goal_today × (1 + inflation)^years

A $100,000 goal in ten years at 3% inflation becomes $100,000 × 1.0310 = $134,392. The extra $34,392 is not a fee or a mistake — it is the same basket of goods at a higher price. Anyone who saves exactly $100,000 over a decade and calls it done has funded about 74% of the goal.

Step 2 — solve for the monthly deposit

Your existing savings grow at the return rate, and the deposits form an annuity. Setting the future value equal to the inflated goal and solving for the payment gives:

FV = PV(1 + r)^n + PMT × [((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r]

where r is the monthly return (1 + annual)^(1/12) − 1 and n is the number of months. With a 6% return, $15,000 grows to about $26,863 over ten years, leaving $107,529 to be funded from deposits.

Two details change the answer more than people expect. Deposits at the start of each month earn one extra month of growth on every payment, which trims the required amount by a few percent over a decade. And if the return is below inflation, the real return is negative — your deposits are fighting a rising price with a shrinking balance in real terms.

Choosing defensible assumptions

Bond fund return4% – 5%Intermediate government and investment-grade credit, held to duration.
Diversified equity return6% – 8%Long-run nominal, before fees. Volatile year to year; unsuitable for short horizons.
Account fees0.1% – 1.5%Subtract from the return, not added separately. A 1% fee over 20 years costs about 18% of the final balance.

Match the assumption to the horizon

Under three years, use a cash rate. A goal you cannot afford to miss — a house deposit, a tax bill — should not depend on an equity market that can drop 20% in a quarter. Over ten years or more, a diversified portfolio is the more defensible assumption, but expect the path to be uneven. The calculator's single return figure hides that volatility; it is a planning average, not a promise.

Practical order of operations

  1. Emergency fund first. Three to six months of essential spending in cash, before any goal that involves risk.
  2. Take the employer match. An employer contribution is an immediate, risk-free return that no market assumption can beat.
  3. Clear high-interest debt. Paying off a 20% credit card is a guaranteed 20% return. No savings product competes with that.
  4. Then fund the goal. With the above handled, the monthly deposit above is realistic rather than aspirational.

Where the model is optimistic

  • Tax. Returns here are pre-tax. Interest is taxed as it is received in most jurisdictions, which cuts the effective compounding rate.
  • Fees. A platform fee, fund expense ratio, and trading spread all reduce the return before you see it.
  • Irregular deposits. Real life skips months. A plan that assumes 120 perfect deposits out of 120 is fragile.
  • Sequence. A fixed average return cannot show a bad year arriving just before you need the money. That is why the last two years of a goal should be de-risked.

A simple robustness test: re-run the numbers with the return cut by two percentage points. If the monthly amount is still affordable, the plan survives a bad decade. If it is not, the goal needs a longer horizon, a smaller scope, or a larger income — no assumption will fix that.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my goal need to be higher than today's price?

The price rises while you save. At 3% a year, $100,000 today is about $134,400 in ten years and about $180,600 in twenty. Saving the current price leaves you short by exactly the inflation component.

What inflation rate should I use?

2.5% to 3% is a common long-run planning figure for developed economies. Use more for housing, education, and healthcare, which have run above general inflation. Re-run the projection when conditions change rather than trusting a single average.

Should I subtract inflation from my return?

Do it once, not twice. This calculator keeps the goal in today's money, inflates it to the target date, and applies the nominal return to savings. Deflating the return and inflating the goal double-counts and overstates the deposit required.

What is a realistic return assumption?

Cash tracks the policy rate, bonds lock in a known nominal yield, and diversified equities have returned roughly 7% nominal before fees over long periods with large swings. For goals under three years, assume a cash rate.

Does this account for tax on the interest?

No, the returns are pre-tax. Reduce the rate by your marginal tax on the interest portion for a conservative plan, or use a tax-advantaged account.

How do I handle irregular income?

Divide annual savings capacity by twelve, target a figure you can always meet in a bad month, and treat surplus months as prepayment. A lump sum can be added to the existing-savings field.
