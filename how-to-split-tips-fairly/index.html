How to Split Tips Fairly

A tip pool is a small redistribution system that runs on trust and a formula. Get the formula wrong, or keep it unwritten, and the pool becomes the most reliable source of conflict in the building.

Last updated 18 November 2026

Pick a method before the shift, not after

Every pool argument is really an argument about method, conducted after the money is already on the table. Three methods cover almost every real house.

By hours worked

share = pool × (your hours ÷ total hours)

The default. Transparent, easy to audit, and it rewards the person who stayed until close. Its weakness is that hours are not the same as contribution: two servers can work five hours each while one turns eight tables and the other turns three.

By points

points = hours × role weight
share = pool × (your points ÷ total points)

Points fix the role problem. A bartender on 1.25 points an hour and a server on 1.0 can work the same shift and finish with different amounts, which recognises that some roles generate or protect more of the pool. Weights must be written down and applied every shift.

By sales

share = pool × (your sales ÷ total sales)

Used in houses where a percentage of each server's sales goes into a communal pot for support staff. It rewards whoever sold the most, which is fair to strong servers and punishing on a quiet section. Most commonly applied to tip-outs rather than the full pool.

Making the cents add up

Three people splitting $100 exactly get $33.333… each. Round every share to $33.33 and the pool is a cent short, which someone will notice.

The rule that survives scrutiny: calculate exact shares, round each one down to the nearest whole unit, then distribute the leftover units to the largest shares first. The total always lands exactly on the pool amount, and the person who gets the extra cent is the one with the biggest share, which nobody objects to.

Rounding to the nearest cent first and adjusting afterwards is what produces totals that drift by a few cents and arguments that last longer than the shift.

Who is allowed to be in the pool

The arithmetic is the easy half. The legal half varies by country and it is where pools get employers into trouble.

United States

  • A pool may include only employees who customarily and regularly receive tips.
  • Managers and supervisors may not take a share, even a token one, from a pool that includes tipped employees.
  • In states without a tip credit, the employer may not retain any portion of employee tips at all.
  • An automatic service charge is not a tip under federal rules. How it is distributed is a policy decision, not tip law — and some states treat it differently, so the menu wording matters.

United Kingdom

The Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act 2023, in force since October 2024, requires 100% of tips and service charges to be passed to workers, allocated fairly, with a written policy and records. Agency workers are covered. The statutory code sets out what "fair" means and gives workers a route to a tribunal.

Canada and Ireland

Ontario and Quebec have specific rules on pooling and employer deductions; other provinces rely more on employment standards and the contract. Ireland requires electronic tips to be distributed fairly and prohibits them being used to make up contractual wages.

The policy that prevents the argument

A pool without a written policy is a pool that gets renegotiated every time someone feels wronged. Six lines on the staff room wall do more than any amount of goodwill:

  1. The method, named explicitly: hours, points with a published weight table, or sales.
  2. What counts as hours — side work, cleaning, pre-shift, handover.
  3. When the pool is calculated and when it is paid.
  4. Who is excluded: supervisors, owners, anyone not customarily tipped.
  5. How disputes are raised, and by when.
  6. The date the policy was last reviewed.

Show each person their own number, not just the total. A pool that is auditable is trusted; a pool that produces one lump sum on a payslip is suspected, usually correctly, of being wrong somewhere.

Three mistakes that cause most disputes

  • Retroactive weighting. Changing the points table after a shift has been worked, so that the pool redistributes against people who already earned it. This is the fastest way to lose a team.
  • Inconsistent hour recording. If side work counts for some people and not others, the pool is not being divided by the stated method, whatever the policy says.
  • Managers in the pool. Even a small percentage, even "just to cover the bar". It is unlawful in the US and corrosive everywhere.

Frequently asked questions

Should tips be split by hours worked or equally?

By hours, in almost every case. Equal splits are simpler and can work for a small team with identical shifts, but they penalise whoever takes the long or quiet shift, which pushes people away from exactly the shifts a house needs covered.

What role weight should a bartender get?

1.2 to 1.3 is the usual range. Above 1.5 the weight starts to dominate the hours and generates more resentment than it resolves. Whatever you choose, publish it and apply it to every shift without exception.

Can a manager take part in a tip pool?

Not in the US, under federal rules. Managers and supervisors are excluded from pools that include customarily tipped employees. In states without a tip credit the employer may not keep any part of employee tips at all.

Is a 20% service charge the same as a tip?

Not under US federal law. An automatic service charge is employer revenue, and how it is distributed is a policy matter rather than tip law. Some states treat it differently, so the wording on the menu and the policy behind it both matter.

How do I handle a tip pool when someone leaves mid-shift?

Pay them for the hours actually worked and recalculate the pool on the remaining hours. Do not hold their share in the pool for the rest of the night, and do not redistribute it retroactively — both create the kind of grievance that outlasts the shift.
