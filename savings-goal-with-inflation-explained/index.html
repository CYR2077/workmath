Why Your Savings Goal Needs an Inflation Adjustment

Saving $100,000 for a goal that costs $100,000 today feels like a plan. Ten years from now that money buys about three quarters of it. The gap is not a fee or a mistake — it is the price of the thing you are saving for.

Last updated 18 November 2026

The two-step calculation

Most savings calculators ask for a future amount and treat it as fixed. That is the wrong frame for anything you will actually spend later, because the thing you are buying gets more expensive while you save.

Step 1 — inflate the goal to the date you need it

nominal goal = goal today × (1 + inflation)^years

$100,000 in ten years at 3% becomes $100,000 × 1.0310 = $134,392. The extra $34,392 is the same basket of goods at a higher price.

Step 2 — solve for the monthly deposit

Existing savings grow at the return rate, and the deposits form an annuity:

FV = PV(1 + r)^n + PMT × [((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r]

With $15,000 already saved at 6%, that balance grows to about $26,863 over ten years, leaving $107,529 to fund from deposits — roughly $662 a month.

The double-counting mistake

This is the most common error in DIY retirement and savings maths, and it goes in both directions.

Option A: state the goal in today's money, inflate it to the target date, and apply the nominal return to your savings. Correct.

Option B: state the goal in today's money, keep it unadjusted, and apply the real return (nominal minus inflation) to your savings. Also correct — it produces the same answer, because the arithmetic is equivalent.

What fails: inflating the goal and deflating the return. That counts inflation twice and overstates the deposit you need by a wide margin, which is how people talk themselves out of goals that were affordable.

Which inflation rate for which goal

GoalReasonable rateWhy
General savings target2.5% – 3.0%Roughly the long-run target in developed economies
House deposit3% – 5%Housing has run above general inflation in most supply-constrained cities
Education4% – 6%Tuition has historically outpaced general prices
Healthcare3% – 5%Consistently above general inflation in most systems
A car in 3 years2% – 3%Short horizon; depreciation and model changes matter more

Using the general rate for a house deposit is the most common and most expensive shortcut. A 1% difference over fifteen years moves the target by roughly 16%.

Real return is the number that matters

real return ≈ (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

A 6% nominal return against 3% inflation is a real return of about 2.91%. That is the rate at which your purchasing power actually grows, and it is the only figure that tells you whether the plan improves your position or merely keeps pace.

Note what happens when the return is below inflation: the real return is negative, and every deposit is fighting a rising price with a shrinking balance. Cash in a low-rate account during a high-inflation period produces exactly this, which is why "safe" savings can still lose ground.

The assumptions that quietly break the plan

  • Tax. Returns are usually quoted pre-tax. Interest is taxed as it is received in most jurisdictions, which cuts the compounding rate.
  • Fees. A 1% annual fee over 20 years removes roughly 18% of the final balance. It is the single most controllable drag.
  • Constant returns. A fixed average cannot show a bad year arriving just before you need the money. That is why the final two years of any goal should be de-risked.
  • Perfect deposits. A plan assuming 120 deposits out of 120 is fragile. Target a figure you can meet in a bad month.

A useful robustness test: re-run with the return cut by two percentage points. If the monthly amount is still affordable, the plan survives a bad decade. If it is not, the goal needs a longer horizon, a smaller scope, or a larger income — no assumption will fix that.

Order of operations

Inflation adjustment is the arithmetic; where the money sits is the strategy, and the order matters more than the rate.

  1. Emergency fund first. Three to six months of essential spending in cash, before anything involving risk.
  2. Take the employer match. An employer contribution is an immediate, risk-free return no market assumption beats.
  3. Clear high-interest debt. Paying off a 20% card is a guaranteed 20% return.
  4. Then fund the goal. With the above handled, the monthly deposit is realistic rather than aspirational.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my goal need to be higher than the price today?

Because the price rises while you save. At 3% a year, $100,000 today is about $134,400 in ten years and about $180,600 in twenty. Saving the current price leaves you short by exactly the inflation component.

Should I subtract inflation from my investment return?

Do it once, not twice. Either inflate the goal and use the nominal return, or keep the goal in today's money and use the real return. Doing both double-counts and overstates the deposit required.

What inflation rate should I use?

2.5–3% for general goals in developed economies, higher for housing, education, and healthcare which have run above general inflation. Re-run the projection when conditions change rather than trusting one long-run average.

Does the calculator account for tax?

No, returns are pre-tax. Reduce the rate by your marginal tax on the interest portion for a conservative plan, or use a tax-advantaged account and ignore the adjustment.

What if inflation is higher than my return?

Then your real return is negative and the balance is losing purchasing power while it grows in nominal terms. In that case a cash-like account is not a savings strategy — it is a slower loss, and the plan needs a different vehicle or a longer horizon.

How often should I re-run the numbers?

Once a year, and whenever inflation or your income changes materially. A projection that is never revisited is a guess that has been left alone for too long.
