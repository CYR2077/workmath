The formula, and the part everyone gets wrong

profit needed = income ÷ (1 − tax rate)
revenue needed = profit needed + business expenses
billable hours = weeks × hours per week × billable %
hourly rate = revenue needed ÷ billable hours

Take a target of $70,000 net, $9,000 of expenses, a 30% tax and contribution rate, 46 weeks at 40 hours with 60% billable:

  • Profit required: $70,000 ÷ 0.70 = $100,000
  • Revenue required: $100,000 + $9,000 = $109,000
  • Billable hours: 46 × 40 × 0.60 = 1,104 hours
  • Minimum rate: $109,000 ÷ 1,104 = $98.73 per hour

Now compare that to the intuitive calculation almost everyone starts with: $70,000 ÷ 2,080 hours = $33.65. The two numbers differ by a factor of three, and the gap is not padding — it is expenses, tax, holiday, and the 40% of the week that earns nothing.

Why the billable share matters more than the rate

Utilisation is the strongest lever in the whole calculation. At 60% you need $98.73 an hour for the same income. At 75% you need $78.99. At 50% you need $118.48. Improving how you spend unbillable time — batching admin, templating proposals, refusing unpaid discovery — is often worth more than a rate increase, and it does not require renegotiating with anyone.

What a salary really costs an employer

The comparison freelancers most often make — old salary divided by hours — understates the true cost by a wide margin. An employer typically adds pension contributions, health insurance, paid holiday, sick pay, equipment, software licences, training, and payroll taxes on top of the salary figure. Add 20% to 40% for benefits and overhead, then remember that only about 60% of your self-employed hours are billable. That is why a $60,000 salaried role and a $95-an-hour freelance rate can produce similar take-home pay.

Pricing beyond the floor

The calculator gives you a floor. The price you quote is a separate decision, and it should account for:

  • Value, not time. If the work unlocks $200,000 of revenue for the client, an hourly rate that reflects your hours is leaving money on the table. Fixed pricing captures that.
  • Urgency and risk. Weekend delivery, tight deadlines, and indemnified work cost more. Saying so is normal.
  • Scope certainty. The less defined the brief, the more contingency belongs in the number, or the more you should be charging hourly.
  • Payment terms. Net 60 from a large client is a loan you are making them. Either price it in or shorten the terms.

Terms that protect the rate

  1. Deposit of 30–50% before work starts, on any new client.
  2. Written scope with an explicit list of what is not included.
  3. A change-request clause: new scope, new quote, no silent absorption.
  4. Late payment interest stated on the invoice, and work pausing past terms.
  5. Rate review annually, with 30–60 days' notice to existing clients.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of a freelancer's week is billable?

Usually 50–70%. Proposals, invoicing, marketing, bookkeeping, and client calls that nobody pays for directly consume the rest. 60% is a realistic planning figure; 100% is how people price themselves into burnout.

Should I charge hourly, daily or per project?

Hourly when scope is unclear, day rate for longer engagements, fixed price when scope is genuinely defined and you can capture the value of your efficiency. Your hourly figure is the internal floor for all three.

How do taxes work for freelancers?

In the US you pay both halves of Social Security and Medicare — about 15.3% on 92.35% of net profit — plus income tax, usually via quarterly estimates. In the UK a sole trader pays income tax and Class 4 NI through Self Assessment; a limited company pays corporation tax with salary and dividends. Enter the combined effective rate, not just income tax.

How much should I add for non-payment risk?

Budget 5–10% on top, take a 30–50% deposit from new clients, state late-payment terms in writing, and stop work when an invoice passes terms. Chasing money is unbillable time.

Why is my rate higher than my old salary divided by hours?

Because the employer was paying pension, insurance, holiday, sick leave, equipment, and software, and because only part of your week is billable. Dividing a salary by 2,080 hours is the most common pricing error in freelancing.

Should I raise my rate for existing clients?

Yes, annually. Announce 30–60 days ahead, apply it to new work rather than already-quoted work, and give a clear date. 5–10% a year is normal and rarely loses clients who value the work.
